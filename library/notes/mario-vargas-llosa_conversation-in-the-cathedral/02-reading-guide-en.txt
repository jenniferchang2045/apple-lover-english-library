Conversation in the Cathedral
Original reading guide

Use this after or beside a legal English edition. Write answers in your own words.


1. CAST (LEARN THESE FIRST)

Santiago Zavala (Zavalita)
  Who: Son of a Lima businessman; later a newspaperman.
  Why: The book's conscience and its failure. He wanted a clean country and a clean self. He gets neither.

Ambrosio
  Who: Chauffeur, later a man living by odd, often dirty work.
  Why: The other half of the bar conversation. He knows the family from below.

Fermin Zavala
  Who: Santiago's father; connected businessman.
  Why: Proof that "decent society" and the regime can share a dinner table.

Cayo Bermudez
  Who: Security / political-police figure of the Odria years.
  Why: The face of organized humiliation. Power here is paperwork, files, and appetite.

Hortensia (the Muse) and the women around the elite
  Who: Mistresses, wives, women who are used as currency.
  Why: The sexual economy of the dictatorship: secrets, jealousy, disposal.

Ana
  Who: Santiago's partner in his later, smaller life.
  Why: The private cost of public disillusion. Love does not automatically redeem politics.

Carlitos and the newsroom
  Who: Colleagues in the press.
  Why: Journalism as a job under pressure: what you print, what you bury, what you drink away.

You will meet more names. If a name appears twice in different years, ask: same person, or the same type of person?


2. STRUCTURE (WHY THE PAGES FEEL CROWDED)

Think of the novel as one long afternoon that contains many earlier rooms.

- Present frame: Santiago and Ambrosio in the bar called La Catedral.
- Past layers: ministries, houses in Lima, Cayo's offices, parties, the newspaper, provincial posts.
- Technique: two or more dialogues are braided. A question in 1963 may be answered by a scene from 1950.

This is not a puzzle novel with one correct timeline you must reconstruct to "win." Reconstructing helps, but the feeling of overlap is the argument: under Odria, private memory and public history are both contaminated. People remember what is useful.

Reading tip: when you get lost, find the "you" and the "I." Who is being addressed? A lover, a boss, a father, a chauffeur? The grammar of address is often a better compass than the chapter number.


3. PLOT WITHOUT SPOILING THE SENTENCES

A full plot dump would still miss the book. Here is the spine, in original paraphrase:

Santiago grows up with money, Catholic schooling, and a father who expects him to be useful. He flirts with left-wing hope and with the idea of breaking from his class. The dictatorship, and the compromises of the people he loves, teach him that Peru can be run as a system of favors. He does not become a hero of the resistance. He becomes a man who understands too much and changes too little.

Ambrosio's path runs through service. He drives, waits, listens, and is asked to do things that cannot be said at the front door. His loyalty is not abstract; it is payroll, fear, affection, and shame twisted together. When he and Santiago talk in the bar, they are not exchanging "news." They are trying to see whether two lives -- the son's and the servant's -- were broken by the same machine.

Around them, Cayo Bermudez's world shows how a regime feeds: files, blackmail, parties, and the destruction of people who know too much. Fermin's world shows how a regime is financed and socially accepted. The novel keeps asking who cleaned their hands, and who only washed the visible part.

The ending is not a courtroom verdict. It is a moral temperature: how cold you can become and still recognize your own name.


4. THEMES TO TRACK (WRITE ONE EXAMPLE EACH)

1. Complicity, not only oppression
   Who benefits from looking away? Include people who tell themselves they are "only doing their job."

2. Class as a soundproof wall
   Santiago and Ambrosio share hours in a car and still do not share a country. Where does speech stop being honest?

3. The body as politics
   Sex, humiliation, and protection are instruments. When a scene feels only "scandalous," ask who holds the file.

4. Language after cynicism
   Newspapers, police reports, family lectures, bar talk. Which language still tries to tell the truth?

5. The famous national question
   English readers usually meet a blunt version of Santiago's question about when Peru went wrong. Do not treat it as a trivia quote. Decide whether the book answers with a date, a person, or a habit.

6. Failed adulthood
   Santiago is not a teenager for long, but he keeps a teenager's all-or-nothing ethics until it collapses into fatigue. That collapse is one of the book's saddest inventions.


5. ENGLISH-STUDY NOTES

These are original explanations, not extracts.

Pattern: unmarked dialogue
  What to do: Read aloud. Change your voice when the social rank changes.

Pattern: long coordinate sentences
  What to do: Find the verbs first. Then find who owns each verb.

Pattern: period slang and names
  What to do: Keep a personal glossary. One line per name is enough.

Pattern: free indirect feeling
  What to do: Ask: is this the narrator, or Santiago judging himself?

Pattern: repetition of nicknames (Zavalita, etc.)
  What to do: Nickname = intimacy, mockery, or both. Note who is allowed to use it.

Useful word families to collect as you read: regime, informer, mistress, newsroom, chauffeur, ministry, blackmail, cynicism, complicity, resignation. Write your own example sentence for each. Do not copy sentences from the novel.


6. STUDY QUESTIONS

Answer in 8-12 sentences of your own English.

1. Is Santiago a victim of Peru, or one of the people who let Peru become what it is? Give two moments that pull in opposite directions.

2. What does Ambrosio know that Santiago's education cannot teach him?

3. If you removed Cayo Bermudez, would the Zavala family still be guilty? Of what?

4. Why is the conversation set in a cheap bar, not in a ministry or a living room?

5. The book is often called a "total novel" of a dictatorship. What does it include that a history textbook would omit? What does it refuse to simplify?

6. Would you trust Santiago as a journalist? Why or why not?


7. A ONE-WEEK READING PLAN (400+ PAGE EDITIONS)

Day 1
  Introduction in this pack + first bar pages. Character cards for Santiago and Ambrosio.

Days 2-3
  Early family / political world. Add Fermin and Cayo to the cards.

Days 4-5
  Middle movements: police, press, private lives. Write a one-page timeline in your words.

Day 6
  Finish. Do not rush the last conversations.

Day 7
  Mind map + three study questions. Then, if you wish, reread only the opening and closing bar scenes.
